# 2026-27 Massachusetts Regulatory Horizon: Body-Shop Labor Rates, Data Privacy, and the FAIR Plan Trigger

**Research brief for MAPFRE U.S. growth strategy engagement**
**As of: July 2, 2026.** All figures carry their own as-of dates. Prepared from public sources; inline citations resolve to the numbered Sources list at the end.

---

## Executive summary

1. **Auto body labor rates.** The statutorily mandated Auto Body Labor Rate Advisory Board filed its final report on December 22, 2025 **without a consensus recommendation** (adopted 7-6, one absent), attaching instead nine competing individual-member recommendations [1][2]. The $55/hour-floor vehicle, **S.797 (Sen. Michael Moore)**, was reported favorably by the Joint Committee on Financial Services and referred to **Senate Ways and Means on January 15, 2026**, where it still sits as of July 2, 2026 — the furthest a rate-floor bill has advanced this session, but not yet to a floor vote, and formal sessions end July 31, 2026 [3][4][19]. Using the Automobile Insurers Bureau (AIB) analysis embedded in the Board's report, a mandated $55 floor against a carrier paying ~$46-49/hour implies roughly a **+12% to +20% increase in paid body-labor rates**, translating (on AIB's own assumptions) to approximately **$22-$36 of added annual loss cost per insured vehicle, or ~1.8-2.9% of the statewide average all-coverages premium of $1,234** — a derived estimate, detailed in Section 1.3 [2].
2. **Data privacy.** The Massachusetts Data Privacy Act is **not yet law**. The Senate passed S.2619 in the fall of 2025 and the House unanimously passed its rewrite, H.5479, on June 4, 2026; a six-member **conference committee was appointed in mid-June 2026** and had not reported a compromise as of July 2, 2026 [5][6][7][8]. Both versions are **opt-in for sensitive data (which includes precise geolocation)**, which is the provision that matters for telematics; they differ on whether sensitive-data *sales* are banned outright (Senate) or permitted with affirmative consent except precise geolocation (House), on a private right of action (House only, for "large data holders"), and on effective dates (Jan. 1, 2027 Senate vs. July 1, 2027 House) [6]. Under the Legislature's new joint rules, a conference deal can be enacted **after** July 31, 2026, any time up to the end of the two-year session on January 5, 2027 [19]. Separately, the **Data Access Law (right-to-repair) litigation is in court-encouraged settlement/mediation talks**: after a skeptical First Circuit oral argument on February 3, 2026, Auto Innovators and the Massachusetts AG agreed in February 2026 to explore a negotiated resolution; no settlement had been announced as of July 2, 2026 [9][10].
3. **FAIR Plan (MPIUA).** The statutory **two-year ban on FAIR Plan rate-increase filings expired in March 2026** [11]. As of July 2, 2026, **no MPIUA rate filing or DOI rate hearing has surfaced in public sources** — the DOI public-hearings docket shows no MPIUA proceeding in 2026 — so the "trigger" the product-adjacent brief looks for has **not yet fired**, though all preconditions are in place: FAIR Plan enrollment jumped to 173,000+ policies in FY2024 (largest one-year jump in two decades), it wrote **8.7% of 2024 Massachusetts home premium**, ~40% of Cape & Islands homes are now FAIR Plan-insured, and voluntary-market nonrenewals roughly quadrupled from 2022 to 2024 [11][12][13]. No formal depopulation/take-out program has been announced.
4. **Rating-factor bans / MAIP.** **No live 2026 initiative would loosen** Massachusetts' statutory bans (credit, sex, age except 65+, occupation, income, education, homeownership) or restructure the MAIP subsidy/credit mechanics. The only live legislative energy runs the *other* way — Sen. Payano's S.820 would further cap territorial (ZIP-code) rating weight — and it has not advanced beyond committee [14][15][16]. The segmentation constraints the strategy assumes fixed are, for the 2026-27 horizon, **safely assumed fixed**.

## 1. Auto body labor rates: from a no-consensus report to a live $55 floor bill

### 1.1 The Advisory Board's December 2025 report — what it found and what it refused to say

The Auto Body Labor Rate Advisory Board — created by Section 292 of Chapter 238 of the Acts of 2024 (the "Mass Leads Act") and required to recommend a "fair and equitable" labor rate by December 31, 2025 — held ten public meetings during 2025 and filed its final report on **December 22, 2025** [2][17]. The decisive facts:

- **No consensus.** At its November 24, 2025 meeting the 14-member board (which includes **Peter Smith, Manager of Material Damage Auto Claims at MAPFRE Insurance**, one of three AIB-selected insurer seats) could not agree on a recommendation; it attached nine individual member recommendations as Appendix H instead. The final report itself was adopted only **7 in favor, 6 opposed, 1 absent** [2].
- **Insurer survey (16 of 17 insurers ≥1% share, ~95% of the MA private passenger market, 2025 data):** prevailing **body-repair rates paid ranged $43-$55/hour** with a market-share-weighted average of **$49**; refinishing also averaged $49; mechanical averaged $55 (range $45-$80); structural $46; frame $50 [2].
- **Shop survey (476 usable responses, 32% response rate):** shops *charge* a mean of **$68/hour for body labor** (median $65) and **$108/hour for mechanical** (median $105) — a $19+/hour gap versus what insurers pay for body work [2].
- **Rhode Island benchmark:** RI's regulator-collected weighted-average insurer-paid body rate is **~$54/hour**, above Massachusetts' $49 [2].
- The Massachusetts Insurance Federation's executive director confirmed on the record that **no U.S. state has established a minimum auto body labor rate** — a mandated floor would be a national first [2].
- Context repeatedly cited in trade coverage: the state's reference labor rate of ~$40/hour dates to 1988 and the 2022 Special Commission had noted an inflation-adjusted equivalent of ~$78/hour [1][4][2].

**Implication:** the report resolved nothing substantively but removed the Legislature's excuse to wait — the "study" phase is over, and the data on the record (a $49 paid average vs. $54 in Rhode Island and $68 charged) is favorable to rate-floor proponents.

### 1.2 Where the $55-floor legislation stands (as of July 2, 2026)

| Bill | Sponsor | Core mechanism | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S.797** (194th G.C.), "An Act reforming auto body labor rates" | Sen. Michael O. Moore | Permanent Auto Body Labor Rate Advisory Board + annual survey; Commissioner of Insurance must set a minimum hourly labor rate for insurance claims of **not less than $55/hour**, effective 30 days after being set | Reported **favorably** by Joint Committee on Financial Services; referred to **Senate Ways and Means 1/15/2026**; no further action as of 7/2/2026 [3][4] |
| Predecessor (193rd G.C.): S.688 / S.2643 | Moore | Similar advisory-board/rate structure | Did not become law in the 2023-24 session [4] |

Procedural reality: under the Legislature's calendar, **July 31, 2026 is the last day of formal sessions**; under the new joint rules only bills already in *conference* by that date can be finished later in the year [19]. S.797 has not passed either chamber, so it must clear Senate Ways and Means, the Senate floor, and the full House within July 2026 — possible (labor-rate bills are politically popular with the 1,400+ shop constituency) but a compressed window. **Most likely outcomes:** (a) it dies in Ways and Means and is refiled in 2027, with the December 2025 report as ammunition; or (b) it is attached to a larger economic-development vehicle, as the Advisory Board itself was in 2024 [2][17]. Either way, a statutory floor is a **2027-28 risk more than a 2026 one**, but the direction of travel is clearly toward intervention.

### 1.3 Loss-cost impact of a $55 floor on a carrier paying ~$46-49/hour

The best public quantification is the **AIB analysis attached to the Board's report (Appendix G)**. AIB assumed: current insurer-paid labor rate of **$44.99/hour**, labor = **36% of total repair cost**, and an average all-coverages premium of **$1,234 per vehicle**. On those assumptions [2]:

| Mandated rate | Increase vs. $44.99 | AIB-estimated premium impact per vehicle | % of $1,234 avg premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| $47.24 | +$2.25 (+5%) | +$8 | ~0.6% |
| $49.49 | +$4.50 (+10%) | +$16 | ~1.3% |
| $49.99 | +$5.00 (+11%) | +$18 | ~1.5% |
| $54.99 | +$10.00 (+22%) | +$36 | ~2.9% |

AIB's headline: **a $10/hour increase ≈ ~3% increase in consumers' auto premiums** [2].

**Derived estimate for a carrier at $46-49/hour (our arithmetic, clearly flagged as such, applying AIB's linear relationship of ~$3.60 premium impact per $1/hour):** a $55 floor is a **+$6 to +$9/hour** move (+12% to +20% on body/refinish labor), i.e. roughly **+4.4% to +7.2% severity on the labor share of physical-damage repair costs** (36% labor share × 12-20%), or approximately **+$22 to +$32 of loss cost per insured vehicle per year — ~1.8% to 2.6% of the average all-coverages premium**, concentrated in collision, comprehensive, and property damage liability. For a book that is ~80% Massachusetts personal auto, this is a **direct, industry-wide loss-cost shock, not a competitive disadvantage** — every carrier faces it, and it is rate-recoverable under managed competition — but it would arrive with a lag between mandated-cost onset (30 days after the Commissioner sets the rate, under S.797) and the next rate filing cycle [2][3][4]. No public source provides a MAPFRE-specific paid rate; the client's stated $46-49/hour is consistent with the survey's $49 weighted average, which MAPFRE's own market weight heavily influences as the state's largest personal auto writer. We could not source a carrier-level rate figure publicly; the estimate above should be re-run on internal severity data.

Second-order effects worth pricing: a floor set by survey/commissioner could **ratchet annually** (S.797's board reports every December 31); shops argue rates should be $70-100+ (the Board's economist member proposed a "fair and equitable centerpoint" near **$83/hour** [1]); and a floor at $55 would compress the negotiating value of MAPFRE's referral/preferred-shop network, since the floor applies regardless of program participation [2].

## 2. Data privacy and telematics: the MDPA is in conference, not reconciled; right-to-repair is in settlement talks

### 2.1 Status correction: there is no "reconciled" Act yet

The premise that H.5479 and S.2619 have been reconciled is **premature as of July 2, 2026**. Timeline [5][6][7][8]:

- Senate passed **S.2619** unanimously (40-0) in fall 2025 [8].
- House passed **H.5479** (its redraft) unanimously on **June 4, 2026** [5][7].
- Senate non-concurred; a six-member **conference committee was appointed mid-June 2026** (House side led by Rep. Michael Moran / Rep. Farley-Bouvier) [5][8].
- No conference report as of July 2, 2026. Business groups (AIM) are actively lobbying the conferees, principally against the House's private right of action [8].
- Under the 2025 joint-rules reform, a conference compromise **can be enacted after July 31, 2026**, any time before the session ends January 5, 2027 — so passage in late 2026 is a live scenario [19].

### 2.2 What each version does to telematics-relevant data (conference will land between these poles)

| Provision | House H.5479 (6/4/2026) | Senate S.2619 (fall 2025) | Telematics relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consent standard | Opt-in "affirmative consent": clear, freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous | Same, plus must respond to a specific controller request; anti-dark-pattern language | UBI enrollment flows must be true opt-in; bundled ToS consent will not qualify [6] |
| Sensitive data | Includes **precise geolocation**, biometric, health, etc.; adds union membership, veteran status, "health and wellness" data | Includes precise geolocation, etc. | Telematics location traces are sensitive data under both [6] |
| Sale/sharing of sensitive data | **Outright ban on sale of precise geolocation**; other sensitive data salable only with separate affirmative consent | **Total ban on all sensitive-data sales** | Either version kills monetization/sharing of driving-location data with third parties (data brokers, lead-gen); first-party rating use survives with consent [6][7] |
| Private right of action | Yes, against "large data holders" (≥2M consumers' data, or sensitive data of ≥200k) via Ch. 93A | No — AG-only enforcement | MAPFRE's MA book (~1M+ policies/customers incl. sensitive data) plausibly clears the 200k-sensitive-consumer prong if geolocation telematics scales — a litigation-exposure question for a large telematics program [6][8] |
| Cure period | None | 60 days, sunsetting June 2027 | Compliance-timing risk [8] |
| Applicability threshold | ≥100k consumers, or ≥$100k revenue from data sales, **or any sensitive-data collection** | ≥60k consumers, or 20k + 20% revenue from sales, or reproductive/sexual-health data | Any telematics program collecting precise geolocation is in scope regardless of size under the House text [6] |
| Financial-institution exemption | Entity-level exemptions incl. financial institutions; **no explicit insurer/GLBA carve-out identified** | Similar but narrower | Whether an insurer qualifies as an exempt "financial institution" is unresolved in public analyses — a key item to track in the conference report [6] |
| Effective date | July 1, 2027 | Jan. 1, 2027 (thresholds June 1, 2027) | Compliance build window of roughly 6-12 months post-enactment [6] |

**Net for telematics strategy:** in every plausible conference outcome, a Massachusetts UBI/telematics program remains legal but must be built **opt-in-first, first-party-only, with no sale or third-party sharing of location data**, honoring deletion/access rights. The binding uncertainty is the **private right of action** — if the House version survives, class-action exposure attaches to large-scale geolocation collection, raising the cost of a telematics launch designed around data breadth rather than data minimization [6][8].

### 2.3 Right-to-repair Data Access Law: settlement talks, not resolution

- **Feb. 3, 2026:** First Circuit oral argument in *Alliance for Automotive Innovation v. Attorney General*. The panel was openly skeptical of aspects of the 2020 ballot law (the inter-operable open-access telematics platform requirement) and of whether the state's proposed compliance path — disabling telematics — squares with federal safety policy; the court **urged mediation** among automakers, the AG, NHTSA, and independent repairers, asking for a status report within three to four weeks [9].
- **Feb. 10, 2026:** Auto Innovators and the Massachusetts AG's office **agreed to pause proceedings to consider settlement** [10].
- **NHTSA posture:** after telling automakers in June 2023 not to comply (cybersecurity), NHTSA reversed and has affirmed automakers *can* comply; in the current administration it has continued to shift positions, and its stance remains a live variable in the mediation [10][18].
- **As of July 2, 2026:** no settlement announced in public sources; the First Circuit has not issued a merits decision.

**Relevance to MAPFRE:** the Data Access Law governs *repair* telematics (mechanical data to shops), not insurance telematics, but a settlement that standardizes an open vehicle-data platform in Massachusetts would (a) lower the cost of OEM-independent driving-data acquisition for UBI, and (b) further entrench the repair-shop ecosystem politically — the same coalition pushing the labor-rate floor [9][10].

## 3. FAIR Plan: moratorium lifted, filing not yet made — the trigger is armed but not fired

### 3.1 Rate-increase status

- A **statutory two-year ban on FAIR Plan rate-increase filings expired in March 2026** [11]. (The FAIR Plan last *raised* rates in 2006 and last *filed* in 2013 — a filing regulators denied [11].)
- In January 2026, FAIR Plan general counsel Frank O'Brien **declined to confirm** whether MPIUA would file, saying only "we're constantly evaluating where we are in the marketplace" [11].
- **As of July 2, 2026 we find no evidence a rate filing has been made:** the Division of Insurance public-hearings docket lists no MPIUA proceeding in 2026 (only a January 2026 managed-care hearing), and no trade-press report of a filing exists [20]. **Conclusion: the rate-increase trigger identified in the product-adjacent brief has not yet fired.** Given the mandatory hearing process (AG intervention as of right, State Rating Bureau participation, discovery, testimony), even a mid-2026 filing would not produce approved rates until well into 2027 [20][11].
- Note: FAIR Plan *premiums* rose ~11% from 2019-2023 anyway, via insured-value and exposure drift rather than rate action, and in 2024/2025 MPIUA increased required coverage amounts for new policyholders [11].

### 3.2 Residual-market growth signals (the demand side of the coastal window)

| Metric | Value | As of | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| FAIR Plan policies in force | 173,000+ (vs. 158,660 prior year; peak ~204,000 in 2007) | FY2024 | [13] |
| One-year policy growth | +24,436 policies — largest jump in two decades, first YoY increase since 2017 | 2024 | [12][13] |
| Share of MA home insurance premium | **8.7%** of 2024 written home premium | 2024 (DOI report publ. Dec 2025) | [12] |
| Cape & Islands penetration | **~40% of homes** FAIR Plan-insured (33% in 2023); 39.6% of policies per DOI | 2025/2024 | [12][13] |
| Voluntary-market nonrenewals statewide | 3,483 (2022) → 9,248 (2023) → **13,000+ (2024)** | 2022-2024 | [13] |
| MA home insurance premium volume | ~$3.9B written, **+12.7% vs. 2023** | 2024 | [12] |
| Coastal/Bristol/Plymouth + Cape & Islands share of FAIR Plan book | ~55% of policies (51% in 2020) | ~2025 | [13] |

- **Depopulation/take-out signals: none found.** Massachusetts has no Florida-style statutory take-out program, and no carrier or MPIUA announcement of an organized depopulation initiative appears in public sources through July 2026. Anecdotally, the pressure is the opposite: Cape Cod homeowners are increasingly **going uninsured** ("going bare") as both voluntary and FAIR Plan costs rise [21]. The realistic near-term version of "depopulation" is simply **selective voluntary-market re-entry**: a carrier willing to write wind-exposed coastal risk at adequate rates faces a 173k-policy pool, 40% Cape/Islands penetration, and an insurer of last resort that still charges 2006-era rate levels — meaning the *moment MPIUA files and wins an increase*, the voluntary market's relative price position improves sharply [11][13].
- **Political risk marker:** the FAIR Plan's own leadership frames the growth as market dysfunction ("when you have a large and growing residual market … the market is not as healthy as it should be" — O'Brien) [11], and the AG's office has previously extracted a $350k settlement from MPIUA over wrongful cancellations — any rate proceeding will be contested and politically visible [22].

## 4. Rating-factor bans and MAIP: constraints are fixed for this horizon

- **Statutory bans remain intact and un-revisited.** MGL c.175 §4E prohibits credit information/credit-based insurance scores in private passenger auto rating, underwriting, and renewal; DOI's Consumer Bill of Rights confirms prohibitions on sex, marital status, race, creed, national origin, religion, age (except the 65+ discount), occupation, income, education, and homeownership [14][15]. We found **no 2025-26 bill, DOI proceeding, or study order proposing to relax any of these** — and no state-level appetite to do so.
- **The live legislative energy points toward more restriction, not less.** Sen. Pavel Payano's **S.820** (194th General Court, "reducing racial and socioeconomic inequities in auto insurance premium pricing") would cap the weight of a driver's own territory at 75% (25% statewide average), echoing his 193rd-session bill; it sat in the Joint Committee on Financial Services after an October 14, 2025 hearing and has not advanced as of July 2026 [16][23]. If it ever passed, it would *compress* territorial segmentation — mildly negative for an urban/ethnic-agent-anchored book's pricing precision, mildly positive for competitiveness in high-rated territories such as Lawrence, Brockton, and Boston neighborhoods.
- **MAIP mechanics unchanged.** CAR's MAIP continues to operate on the established assignment/quota-share and Subsidy Clearinghouse structure (positive/negative subsidies by class, territory, coverage); 2025-26 rate manuals and 2026 Rules of Operation show **no structural change to the credit/subsidy architecture**, and no DOI docket proposes one [24][25]. The only enacted change in the compulsory framework is Chapter 275 of the Acts of 2024 (H.5111), which raised Massachusetts' compulsory minimum limits for the first time since 1988 (effective for policies issued/renewed from July 1, 2025) — a modest tailwind to average premium and to limits-profile upgrades, already in run-rate [15].
- **Advisory Board seat as an asset:** MAPFRE holds one of the three AIB insurer seats on the labor-rate board (Peter Smith) [2] — useful standing influence if S.797's permanent board becomes law.

**Bottom line for segmentation strategy:** assume the ban set is fixed through at least the 2027-28 session. The differentiation frontier in Massachusetts personal auto therefore remains **telematics/behavioral data (subject to Section 2's opt-in constraints), group/affinity marketing discounts, tenure/account structure, and claims-side cost control** — not new rating variables.

## So what for MAPFRE's growth strategy

1. **Price the labor-rate floor as a 2027 loss-cost scenario, and get ahead of it in filings.** A $55 floor from a $46-49 base is a ~2-3%-of-premium, all-industry severity shock (~$22-36/vehicle on AIB's math) [2]. Because it hits every carrier symmetrically and Massachusetts allows annual rate filings, the real risks are (a) the recognition lag between mandate and approved rates and (b) an annually ratcheting floor under S.797's permanent board. Recommended posture: build the +$6-9/hour scenario into 2027 indications now; use the Advisory Board seat and AIB to push any floor toward survey-indexed, preferred-shop-compatible designs; and treat the shop network less as a rate-negotiation lever (a floor devalues that) and more as a **cycle-time and quality lever**, where MAPFRE's Massachusetts claims density is a genuine scale advantage.
2. **Design the direct/telematics build for the MDPA end-state, not the status quo.** Whatever emerges from conference will be opt-in for geolocation, hostile to any third-party data sharing, and possibly enforceable by private class action against large data holders [6][8]. For a carrier with essentially no direct channel today, this is oddly good news: MAPFRE can architect a telematics program **privacy-native from day one** (first-party, minimized, consent-logged), while incumbents with legacy data-broker plumbing (LexisNexis-style driving-data ecosystems) carry retrofit risk. The compliance clock likely starts at enactment in late 2026 with effectiveness in 2027 — aligning naturally with a 2027 direct-channel/UBI launch window.
3. **The FAIR Plan trigger is armed, not fired — pre-position now.** No MPIUA rate filing exists as of July 2, 2026 [20], but every precondition (expired moratorium, 20 years without a rate change, 173k policies, 8.7% of premium, 40% Cape/Islands penetration, quadrupled nonrenewals) is in place [11][12][13]. The window play: build coastal homeowners appetite (reinsurance, wind deductible design, agency distribution on the Cape/South Coast — where MAPFRE's independent-agent network already sits) **before** an approved FAIR Plan increase makes voluntary pricing look attractive, because the post-approval land-grab will be contested and the DOI hearing process gives 12+ months of visible lead time. Watch items: any MPIUA filing on the DOI docket; the FY2025/FY2026 MPIUA annual reports; legislative reaction (affordability bills) to any filing.
4. **Segmentation constraints are stable — win on what Massachusetts allows.** No rating-factor liberalization is coming [14][15][16]; if anything, territorial rating may compress (S.820). Growth economics in MA personal auto will be decided by claims-cost control (Sections 1 and 2), retention/account rounding through agents, and being early to compliant telematics — not by out-segmenting competitors on new variables.
5. **Legislative calendar discipline.** July 31, 2026 is the formal-session cutoff for anything not yet in conference [19]: track (a) whether S.797 moves out of Senate Ways and Means this month; (b) the MDPA conference report (can land any time to Jan. 5, 2027); (c) the First Circuit mediation status reports. These three dates-to-watch effectively define the 2027 planning assumptions for claims severity, data strategy, and channel build.
