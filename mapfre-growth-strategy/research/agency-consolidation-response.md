# Distribution Plant Under Consolidation: Book Movement, Consolidator Carrier Deals, and the Safeco Recruiting Window

**Research brief for MAPFRE Insurance (The Commerce Insurance Company) growth strategy engagement**
**Prepared:** 2026-07-02. All figures carry as-of dates; where sources conflict, both are shown.

---

## Executive summary

- Massachusetts agency M&A is running at a steady ~30 deals/year (31 in 2025, 30 in 2024, 37 in 2023), but the buyer mix has shifted: the most active 2025 acquirers of MA agencies were *local/regional* consolidators — Kaplansky (now the Alera Group platform), Duffy, DiVirgilio, Deland Gibson — supplemented by national platforms (Hub, Gallagher, World, King Risk, Patriot Growth, Trucordia, Hilb) [1][2].
- The widely quoted "BroadStreet 69 deals" figure is a **national** 2025 count from OPTIS Partners, not a Massachusetts count; the "29" figure matches **Leavitt Group's national** 2025 count. Total MA deals across *all* buyers in 2025 was **31**. No 2025 BroadStreet acquisition of a Massachusetts agency surfaced in trade press. The two numbers reconcile as an attribution error, not a data conflict [1][2][3].
- At ~30 deals/year against MAPFRE's self-reported "nearly 1,300 independent agents in Massachusetts" [4], roughly **2–2.5% of the MA agency plant changes ownership annually** — cumulatively 12–15% of MAPFRE's distribution plant over a 5-year plan horizon.
- **No public trade-press evidence was found of MA personal-auto books being rolled away from regional carriers after acquisition.** The risk is real but inferential: the book-roll machinery is mature and industrialized (Ivans Bookroll, Vertafore Book Roll, Big "I" book-roll handbook) [5][6][7], consolidators openly market "national carrier relationships and better commissions" to sellers (Trucordia) [8], and Liberty Mutual's Safeco executed one of the largest personal-lines book transfers on record with Main Street America in Nov 2024 [9]. Absence of headlines is not absence of movement — book rolls are private, agency-by-agency events.
- Liberty Mutual retired the Safeco brand effective **April 25, 2026** (announced March 3, 2025), folding a ~$13–14B, 22,000-agency IA-channel book under the Liberty Mutual name [10][11][12]. Liberty's US Retail Markets written premium fell **6.4% in 2025** on deliberate underwriting actions [13]; its MA private-passenger-auto share slid from **5.36% (Oct 2024) to 4.98% (Dec 2025)** [14][15]. Official messaging claims agents are "overwhelmingly eager" [12]; comment-section and agency-blog evidence shows skepticism about brand-equity loss and channel conflict with Liberty's direct business [11][16]. **As of July 2026, no competitor recruiting campaign explicitly targeting ex-Safeco agents in New England has been publicly documented** — the window is open and quiet.
- Massachusetts rater economics are idiosyncratic: the state-specific incumbent **Boston Software (WinRater/SinglePoint, ~1,700+ MA agents claimed)** dominates, Vertafore PL Rating supports a 27-carrier MA panel **including MAPFRE for auto and home**, while **EZLynx's MA panel (Jan 2024 grid) does not list MAPFRE** — nor Plymouth Rock or Arbella — and TurboRater's Northeast panels are thin [17][18][19][20]. No vendor publishes MA rater market-share statistics; this must be sized via agent survey.

## 1. Consolidator platforms active in Massachusetts: carrier arrangements and book-movement evidence

### 1.1 Who actually bought Massachusetts agencies in 2025

Agency Checklists (the authoritative MA-market tracker) counted **31 MA agency transactions in 2025** (8 in Q1, 7 in Q2, 7 in Q3, 9 in Q4), vs 30 in 2024 and 37 in 2023 [1]. The named repeat buyers:

|---|---|---|---|

Also active: Hub International, Arthur J. Gallagher, Alera Group directly, Sterling Insurance, Renaissance Alliance (network), and regional mutual-adjacent buyer Deland Gibson [1].

### 1.2 Carrier agreements and preferred-market arrangements

Public specificity here is limited — platform-carrier agreements are commercial secrets — but the pattern is documentable:

- **Trucordia** is the most explicit: its seller-facing marketing promises "access to national carrier relationships and better commissions," and it employs a head of insurance markets and broking (Scott Palladino) responsible for carrier partnerships and MGA/MGU platform development [8][31].
- **BroadStreet** describes providing Core Agency Partners "a vast network of market resources" [30].
- **Alera Group** (Kaplansky's owner) centralizes P&C carrier relationships across a $1.5B-revenue national platform [22].
- The **enabling infrastructure is industrialized**: Ivans Bookroll and Vertafore Book Roll are productized carrier-growth tools that "surface detailed policy information at scale" so carriers can bid for acquired-agency books; the Big "I" publishes a Book Roll Handbook; M&A is explicitly cited by vendors as a driver — "if an acquiring agency has a preferred set of carriers, it might look to consolidate books from its acquired agency" [5][6][7].

### 1.3 Is there public evidence of MA personal-lines books rolling away from regional carriers post-acquisition?

**No — we found no trade-press documentation of a specific MA personal-auto book being rolled off a regional carrier (MAPFRE, Safety, Arbella, Plymouth Rock, Norfolk & Dedham, Quincy Mutual) following an agency acquisition, as of July 2026.** Three honest caveats:

1. Book rolls are private, gradual (executed at renewal), and never press-released by the losing carrier; absence of evidence is weak evidence of absence.
2. The one *documented* mega-movement runs the other way in brand terms but proves scale feasibility: **Main Street America transferred its entire personal-lines book (7 lines, 22 states, ~4,000 agents) to Safeco**, announced Nov 12, 2024, transitions beginning Q2 2025 — Liberty called it "one of the largest book transfer opportunities" in its history [9].
3. The aggregate CAR data is consistent with slow leakage rather than sharp book rolls: Commerce's MA private-passenger-auto share declined from **22.1% (2020) to 19.02% (Dec 2024) to 18.33% (Dec 2025)** — roughly 4 points in five years — while agency-distributed regionals Safety (9.30%), Plymouth Rock (7.88%) and Arbella (7.08%) held ~24% collectively and Progressive (also IA-distributed) exploded from 5.1% to 12.17% [14][15]. The biggest documented book movement in MA personal auto is **toward Progressive through the very same independent agents**, not away from the channel.

## 2. The Safeco brand retirement: measurable fallout in New England

### 2.1 Facts and timeline

- **March 3, 2025**: Liberty Mutual announces single-brand strategy; Safeco (~$13B annual premium, 48 states, 22,000+ agencies) to retire in 2026 [10][11].
- **November 12, 2024** (preceding): Main Street America personal-lines book transfer to Safeco — enlarging the very book about to be rebranded [9].
- **April 25, 2026**: Retirement effective; Safeco now "nearly $14 billion" at sunset; all personal lines sold as Liberty Mutual; agents keep ownership of customer relationships, differentiated IA-channel products, new co-op marketing, loyalty program, "Find an Agent" tools [12][32][33].
- **Liberty FY2025 results** (reported March 2026): net income $6.8B (+55%), combined ratio 88.4; **US Retail Markets written premium down 6.4%** on deliberate portfolio actions, underlying CR 79.9, "record levels of advertising spend," and a return to *sequential* personal-lines PIF growth by year-end; CEO Tim Sweeney: 2026 is "shifting from fixing to building" [13][34].

### 2.2 Massachusetts-specific signal

Liberty Mutual's MA private-passenger-auto position (CAR data): **5.36% share / 225,988 exposures as of Oct 2024 → 4.98% / 247,128 exposures as of Dec 2025** [14][15]. (Note: the share fell while listed exposures rose because the Oct 2024 table covered only top writers; the Dec 2025 total market was 4,958,343 exposures. Trust the share series, which is Agency Checklists' consistent CAR-based metric.) Liberty is the #8 MA auto writer — a ~247k-exposure book sitting with independent agents who just lost the brand they sold for 18 years.

### 2.3 Agent reaction: official vs. observed

- **Official**: Luke Bills (Liberty president of IA distribution): agents "overwhelmingly eager to now leverage the Liberty Mutual brand" after a "year-long process... of agent engagement" [12].
- **Observed skepticism** (Insurance Journal reader comments, March 2025): "policyholders trusted Safeco more than they do Liberty Mutual. The personal lines products at Safeco are significantly better than LM, and at a better price" (Anita Lei); criticism of brand-equity destruction (Tony Wilkinson); a report of California personal-auto cancellations with customers moved to Liberty Mutual paper (JG) [11].
- **Agency-channel commentary** (May 2026): for agencies that built personal-lines books in the 2010s, "Safeco wasn't just a market. It was *the* market"; the unresolved tension is that Liberty Mutual "simultaneously competes directly with independent agents online while claiming to support them" — the Limu Emu brand is a direct-channel brand [16].
- **IA Magazine** (April 29, 2026) framed the transition neutrally but catalogued the agent-retention program Liberty felt obliged to build (co-op dollars, loyalty program, agent-locator advertising) — itself a tell that defection risk is taken seriously internally [33].

### 2.4 Competitor recruiting campaigns and early book movement

**No publicly documented recruiting campaign targeting ex-Safeco agents in New England was found as of July 2026** — no announced appointment drives from Safety, Arbella, Plymouth Rock, Hanover, Vermont Mutual, or Quincy Mutual referencing the Safeco sunset [35]. Nor are there early book-movement signals in public data: the retirement is only ~10 weeks old and the first CAR share reading that could capture it (data through ~June 2026) has not yet been published. This is a genuine white space: the first regional carrier to build a structured "Safeco transition desk" (fast appointments, book-roll tooling, comparable coastal-property appetite) faces no publicly visible competition for it today.

## 3. Comparative raters in Massachusetts and carrier rating connectivity

### 3.1 Rater market share: what is and is not knowable

**No vendor or third party publishes rater market-share figures for Massachusetts.** That figure cannot be sourced publicly and should be sized via MAPFRE's own agent survey or Ivans/AMS census data. What is documentable:

| Rater | MA position (public evidence) | As-of |
|---|---|---|
| **Boston Software WinRater/SinglePoint** | The MA-specialist incumbent since 1995 ("first Windows-based comparative rater in the country"); claims **"1,700-plus independent agents"** on WinRater/SinglePoint with real-time quote-to-issue, plus MA-unique workflow (EVR plate issuance, RMV integration, DEC-page quoting); primary market is Massachusetts [17][36]. Against a total MA independent-agency population on the order of MAPFRE's ~1,300 appointed agencies [4], this implies **dominant MA penetration** | 2025–26 (company claims) |
| **Vertafore PL Rating** | Dedicated MA panel: **27 carriers**; auto panel includes MAPFRE, Safety, Plymouth Rock, Arbella, Progressive, Liberty Mutual, Travelers, Vermont Mutual, National General, Nationwide, Bristol West, Hanover TAP, Tower, and MAIP (residual market) [18] | Current (vendor page, retrieved July 2026) |
| **Applied EZLynx** | MA panel per official carrier grid (Jan 18, 2024): ASI/Progressive, BH Guard, Bristol West, Lemonade, Liberty Mutual, Main Street America, MetLife, Nationwide, NLC, Openly, Progressive, Providence Mutual, **Safeco**, Safety, Stillwater, Travelers, Union Mutual, Universal P&C. **MAPFRE, Plymouth Rock and Arbella are absent.** EZLynx lists MAPFRE only in Connecticut [19][37] | Jan 2024 (grid date) — verify currency before acting |
| **ITC/Zywave TurboRater** | Supports MA, but panels of 20–30 carriers/state skew to Texas/Southeast/Midwest with "lighter carrier panels in some Northeast states" — a marginal MA player [20][38] | 2026 (vendor/review sites) |

Analytical judgment: in MA personal auto, the practical fight is **SinglePoint first, PL Rating second, EZLynx a distant third**; TurboRater is negligible. Confidence: medium (vendor claims, no independent census).

### 3.2 Which MA personal-auto carriers offer real-time rating connectivity

From the panels above (as-of dates noted): **MAPFRE (Commerce), Safety, Plymouth Rock, Arbella, Progressive, Liberty Mutual, Travelers, Vermont Mutual and the MAIP all support real-time auto rating in Vertafore PL Rating** [18]; Boston Software advertises "Real-Time connections with many carriers for rating and submitting" (carrier list not public; MAPFRE's presence on SinglePoint could not be publicly confirmed and should be verified internally) [36]; EZLynx's MA auto panel is thinner and notably **excludes MAPFRE and the other MA regionals except Safety** (as of Jan 2024) [19]. Implication: MAPFRE has rater presence where MA agents live today, but is invisible on the national rater (EZLynx) that consolidator platforms standardize on — precisely the systems an acquired agency migrates to.

## 4. Reconciling the BroadStreet "69 vs 29" Massachusetts deal counts

The two numbers come from different populations and, in one case, a different company:

| Figure | What it actually is | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **69** | BroadStreet Partners' **national** 2025 acquisition count per OPTIS Partners' full-year report (down from 90 in 2024; still the #1 buyer) | OPTIS FY2025 via Agency Checklists, Jan 26, 2026 [2] |
| **29** | **Leavitt Group's national** 2025 count (the only privately held broker in OPTIS's top 10). No BroadStreet figure of 29 appears in any OPTIS period: BroadStreet ran 18 (Q1), 39 (H1), 57 (through Q3), 69 (FY) [2][3][39] | Same reports |
| **31** | Total **Massachusetts** deals in 2025 across **all** buyers (8/7/7/9 by quarter) | Agency Checklists Q4-2025 MA M&A report, Jan 26, 2026 [1] |
| **695** | Total US 2025 deals, −11.7% vs 787 in 2024, ~24% below the prior 5-year average | OPTIS FY2025 [2][3] |

**Conclusion: there is no conflict to reconcile — "69 in MA" is a mis-scoping (national count applied to MA) and "29" is a mis-attribution (Leavitt's national count).** BroadStreet had no publicly reported MA acquisition in 2025; its July 2025 recapitalization (Ethos Capital/BCI/White Mountains alongside Ontario Teachers') is a capital event, not a deal-count event [30].

**Corrected trajectory and what it implies:** MA consolidation is a steady grind, not an avalanche — 37 (2023), 30 (2024), 31 (2025) [1]. Against MAPFRE's "nearly 1,300" MA agencies [4], ~30 ownership changes/year ≈ **2.3% of the plant annually, ~12% over five years** — and the deals skew toward exactly the personal-lines Main Street agencies that hold MAPFRE's book (Kaplansky's tuck-ins, Bearce, Acacia, Charles River). The near-term risk is less "BroadStreet buys Massachusetts" than **local platforms (Kaplansky/Alera, Duffy, DiVirgilio) aggregating MAPFRE's own top agencies into fewer, larger, better-leveraged counterparties** whose ultimate owners hold national carrier agreements.

## So what for MAPFRE's growth strategy

1. **Reframe the threat: it's concentration of negotiating power, not book flight — yet.** No public evidence shows MA personal-auto books rolling off regionals post-acquisition, and CAR data shows share leaking to Progressive *through* agents, not to consolidator-preferred national carriers [14][15]. But the machinery (Ivans/Vertafore book-roll products, Trucordia's "national carrier relationships" pitch, the MSA-to-Safeco precedent) means the switching cost of a 5,000-policy book has collapsed [5][6][8][9]. MAPFRE should treat its top ~100 agencies as M&A-contingent accounts: pre-negotiated **platform-level agreements with Alera/Kaplansky, Duffy, DiVirgilio, World, King Risk and Trucordia** (profit-share aggregation, service-center support, growth bonuses that survive change of control) before the next 30 deals happen, not after.

2. **The Safeco window is real, quantifiable, and — publicly — uncontested.** Liberty holds ~247k MA auto exposures (4.98%, Dec 2025, trending down) [15], shrank US Retail written premium 6.4% in 2025 [13], and just deleted the only IA-native brand its 22,000 agencies had [12]. Documented agent sentiment includes brand-equity grief and channel-conflict distrust of Limu-Emu-branded direct competition [11][16]. No New England carrier has publicly launched a Safeco-transition recruiting program [35]. A MAPFRE "IA-loyalty" campaign — fast appointments, book-roll desk with Ivans Bookroll tooling, MA+coastal-home appetite messaging, and an explicit "we will never compete with you direct" pledge (which MAPFRE's non-functional direct channel makes credible) — could be first to market. The counter-tension: MAPFRE's own growth strategy may eventually want a direct channel; sequencing matters — harvest the Safeco window first.

3. **Fix rater visibility where consolidators standardize.** MAPFRE is present on the MA-native stack (PL Rating confirmed; SinglePoint to be verified) but absent from EZLynx's MA panel as of the Jan 2024 grid [18][19]. Acquired agencies get migrated onto national platforms' standard AMS/rater stacks; if MAPFRE can't be quoted there in real time, erosion becomes mechanical. A modern rating API with EZLynx (and confirmed SinglePoint real-time bind) is defensive infrastructure, not innovation.

4. **The honest bogey is Progressive, and the consolidation story compounds it.** Progressive went 5.1% → 12.17% of MA auto in five years through the same agents MAPFRE relies on [15]. Consolidators optimize for ease-of-quote and commission economics — both Progressive strengths. Every retention lever above (platform deals, Safeco recruiting, rater APIs) should be scored against one metric: share of the *acquired* agency's new-business quotes that still include a MAPFRE rate 12 months post-close.

**Confidence and gaps:** deal counts, market shares, and Safeco timeline are high-confidence (CAR/OPTIS/primary announcements). Rater share, SinglePoint carrier panel, consolidator commission terms, and any actual post-acquisition book-roll losses from MAPFRE's plant are **not publicly knowable** and require internal data or field interviews.
