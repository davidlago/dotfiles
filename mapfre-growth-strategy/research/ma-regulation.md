# Massachusetts Auto Insurance Regulation and the Residual Market
### Research brief for MAPFRE Insurance growth strategy engagement
*Prepared July 2, 2026. All figures carry as-of dates; sources are numbered at the end.*

---

## 1. Executive summary

Massachusetts runs one of the most distinctive personal auto regulatory regimes in the U.S. Since April 2008 it has operated under "managed competition": carriers file their own rates on a file-and-use basis, but a regulation (211 CMR 79.04) bans more rating variables than almost any other state — credit, age (except a mandatory 65+ discount), sex, marital status, occupation, income, education, and homeownership are all off the table [1][7][8]. The residual market (MAIP, administered by Commonwealth Automobile Reinsurers) is small — about 1.7% of the market at 10 months of policy year 2025 — but its burden is distributed unevenly: The Commerce Insurance Company (MAPFRE) carried 30.4% of all MAIP business against an 18.1% voluntary market share, a structural subsidy from MAPFRE to competitors that exploit CAR's credit system [11]. Group marketing deviations under G.L. c.175 §193R are a legally sanctioned, MA-specific channel lever; MAPFRE's exclusive AAA Northeast agreement (>$200M of 2025 premium) expires December 31, 2026 and is already the subject of litigation after AAA began selling a competing carrier's product on January 1, 2026 [5][6]. Telematics is permitted and is effectively the only remaining frontier of pricing sophistication in the state; MAPFRE's DriveAdvisor dates to 2013 while Plymouth Rock now advertises up to 30–40% telematics savings [14][15][16]. The most material pending regulatory items are the July 1, 2025 increase in mandatory minimum limits (already in force), auto body labor-rate legislation, and a comprehensive state data privacy act that passed the House on June 4, 2026 [18][19][20][22].

## 2. Managed competition (post-2008): how rates get approved and what can be priced

### 2.1 The rate approval mechanism

- Until April 2008, the Insurance Commissioner **fixed and established** a single statewide rate set for private passenger auto — the last "fix-and-establish" system in the country. From April 2008, under "managed competition," insurers file their own rates under G.L. c.175E [1][2].
- The system is **file-and-use with a disapproval backstop**: filed rates take effect unless the Commissioner disapproves them. Then-Commissioner Nonnie Burnes described her authority as "limited strictly to disapproving a rate or, under very limited circumstances set forth in the statute, approving it," judged on whether the **overall** rate level is excessive, inadequate, or unfairly discriminatory — not on line-item objections to profit, commission or loss provisions, which the DOI called "irrelevant" under managed competition [1][2].
- The **Attorney General** has standing to trigger hearings on filings deemed excessive, but final decisions rest with the Commissioner [1][2]. In practice the AG intervenes episodically (e.g., the 2008 challenges to the largest writers' initial filings, all of which were ultimately allowed) [2].
- Practical consequence for the current hard market: the DOI approved large increases when loss trends justified them. Per a Boston Globe analysis of DOI filings, the seven largest MA insurers raised rates an average of **more than 12% in 2023** — the largest one-year increase since at least 2015 — including **~26% for GEICO**; by October 2024 the same seven carriers had received approval for a further **~5% average** [3]. Rate adequacy is therefore achievable in MA; the binding constraint is *how* you can segment, not *whether* you can earn an adequate overall level.

### 2.2 Constraints on rating factors (211 CMR 79.04 and c.175 §4E)

Massachusetts bans more rating dimensions than nearly any other state. Under 211 CMR 79.04, private passenger classification plans, rules or rates are subject to disapproval if based in whole or in part on [7][8]:

| Factor | Status in MA | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Credit-based insurance score / credit info | **Banned** (rating, underwriting, renewal) | Statutory ban, G.L. c.175 §4E; MA is one of only ~4 states with a full ban [4][8][9] |
| Age | **Banned** | Except the mandatory rate reduction for insureds 65+ [7][8]; carriers use *years of driving experience* instead [4] |
| Sex / gender identity / sexual orientation | **Banned** | [8] |
| Marital status | **Banned** | [8] |
| Occupation, income, education, homeownership | **Banned** | Also banned as underwriting/tiering factors [8] |
| Race, creed, national origin, religion | **Banned** | [8] |
| Driving record / at-fault accidents | Allowed | Merit rating (SDIP or company plans, 211 CMR 134.00); events older than 6 years cannot be referenced and cannot affect premium beyond ~5 years [8] |
| Years licensed (driving experience) | Allowed | The de facto substitute for age [4] |
| Territory | Allowed but structured | Commissioner publishes designations of **no fewer than 15 territories** [8] |
| Vehicle type, mileage, coverage, telematics behavior | Allowed | Telematics programs operate via filed discounts (Section 5) |
| Group/affinity membership | Allowed | Via approved group marketing deviations, c.175 §193R (Section 4) |

The same list applies to **underwriting and company placement**: an insurer may not decline or slot an applicant into a higher-rated affiliate based on any of the prohibited factors [8].

**What this means for pricing sophistication.** In most states, national direct writers' advantage rests heavily on credit-based scores and thousands of micro-segments built on demographic and financial variables. In Massachusetts, that entire toolkit is unavailable to everyone. The remaining sophistication levers are: (i) driving record and experience; (ii) territory (within a floor of 15 territory definitions); (iii) vehicle/usage characteristics; (iv) **telematics**, the only high-dimensional behavioral signal legally available; and (v) tier/affiliate structures and group deviations built on permissible criteria. This compresses the segmentation gap between a regional incumbent like MAPFRE and Progressive/GEICO relative to other states — but the CAR data (Section 3) and market-share trend show Progressive growing from 5.1% (2020) to 12.17% (December 2025) anyway [10], suggesting the battle has shifted to telematics, brand/price perception, and expense ratio rather than classical rating-plan breadth.

## 3. MAIP: the residual market and each carrier's obligation

### 3.1 Mechanics

- The **Massachusetts Automobile Insurance Plan (MAIP)** is an assigned-risk plan (replacing the old reinsurance-pool CAR mechanism for private passenger business, per the 2004–2008 reform) administered by **Commonwealth Automobile Reinsurers (CAR)** under Rules of Operation approved by the DOI [12][13].
- **Participation is mandatory**: essentially every carrier writing private passenger auto in MA is a CAR member and must accept MAIP assignments [12].
- Drivers who cannot obtain a voluntary policy are assigned via a **real-time quota-share calculation**; each member's quota share is based on its **voluntary private passenger written exposures (car years) over the trailing 12 months**, adjusted monthly, with MAIP business excluded from the base [12][13].
- **Rate protection:** the assigned carrier must charge the **lesser of** the CAR-filed MAIP rate or the rate it would charge that risk voluntarily [12]. MAIP rates are filed by CAR and placed on file by the DOI (most recently for policies effective November 1, 2024 and subsequent) [13].
- **Credits (Rule 29):** members earn credits against their assignment quota for voluntarily writing risks in **11 designated urban credit territories** (Chelsea, Hyde Park, Dorchester, Roxbury, East Boston/Charlestown, Holyoke, Lowell, Springfield, Lynn, Lawrence, Brockton) and for high-risk classes; **take-out credits** (factor 1.0 for one year, reaffirmed by CAR's Actuarial Committee in April 2025) reward removing risks from the pool. Surplus credits can effectively be **sold to competitors** unwilling to take MAIP paper [13][17].
- The old "Clean-in-Three" non-renewal protections were phased out for policies effective April 1, 2014 and later [12].

### 3.2 Size and distribution of the burden (primary CAR data)

CAR's Residual Market Volume Analysis (report dated December 22, 2025; shipments through December 15, 2025; policy year at 10 months) [11]:

| Metric | PY2024 @10 mo. | PY2025 @10 mo. | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Industry total PPA car years | 4,218,133 | 4,251,405 | +0.8% |
| **MAIP car years** | 83,048 | 70,560 | **−15.0%** |
| MAIP as % of market | 2.0% | **1.7%** | — |
| Commerce (MAPFRE) voluntary car years | 774,139 | 756,351 | −2.3% |
| Commerce MAIP car years | 28,891 | 21,433 | −25.8% |
| **Commerce share of all MAIP business** | 34.8% | **30.4%** | — |
| Commerce share of voluntary market | 18.7% | 18.1% | — |

Historical context: the residual market was 4.3% of the state in 2007 and 1.25% in 2018 [17] — MAIP has stayed structurally small since managed competition began, though it swelled during the 2022–24 hard market before shrinking again in 2025 [11][13].

**The strategic problem in the table:** Commerce carries roughly **1.7x its "fair share"** of MAIP business (30.4% of MAIP vs. 18.1% of the voluntary market as of PY2025 at 10 months) [11]. The mechanism is the credit system: carriers such as Progressive, GEICO, Plymouth Rock and Liberty Mutual write voluntary business in the urban credit territories (or buy credits), offsetting their assignment quotas, while the residual burden concentrates on carriers with fewer credits. This is not new: CAR itself proposed phasing out voluntary-writing credits (20% per year over five years) in November 2018 — **MAPFRE and Arbella supported the phase-out; Plymouth Rock, Progressive, GEICO, Liberty Mutual, the agents' association and the AG opposed it — and the Commissioner denied it in April 2019**, having denied a similar 2016 attempt after a Plymouth Rock appeal [17]. Absent a rule change (which the DOI has twice refused), the practical response is to *play the credit game*, not fight it: write voluntary urban/inexperienced-operator business that both earns credits and is priced adequately, and pursue take-out credits.

## 4. Group marketing / affinity discounts

### 4.1 The rules

- G.L. c.175 **§193R** authorizes insurers to offer **group marketing plan rate deviations** — discounted rates to members of qualifying groups (employers, unions, associations, credit unions, **motor clubs**, etc.) [21].
- Deviations must be filed with and approved by the **Division of Insurance annually**; approved deviations are posted by insurer on the DOI website. Most filings come in the first months of each calendar year [21].
- As of the DOI's current posting, insurers with approved auto group deviations include: **Commerce (MAPFRE), Safety, Plymouth Rock, Arbella, GEICO General, Amica, Citizens (Hanover), Norfolk & Dedham, Farmers P&C, Main Street America, and Standard Fire (Travelers)** [21]. Specific group-by-group percentages are only visible in the per-insurer postings; no aggregate public table of discount levels exists (we could not source a market-wide average — this is an open item best answered from DOI filings or SNL rate-filing data).

### 4.2 Who exploits group marketing best — and the AAA fight

- **MAPFRE/Commerce's AAA Northeast relationship is the single largest affinity franchise in the state.** An exclusive joint marketing agreement (JMA) signed in **2007** gives MAPFRE exclusive rights to market auto insurance to AAA members using the AAA emblem with preferential (group-deviated) pricing. The JMA runs through **December 31, 2026** and generated **over $200 million of premium for MAPFRE in 2025** [6]. (For scale: Commerce's total MA PPA book is ~908,833 exposures, 18.33% share, as of December 2025 [10].)
- In **January 2026 MAPFRE sued AAA Northeast** in Massachusetts state court, alleging AAA began selling auto insurance from its affiliated **Motor Club Insurance Company (MCIC)** on **January 1, 2026** — a year before JMA expiry — including AAA-member discounts the JMA reserves to MAPFRE. Claims include breach of contract, implied covenant, unjust enrichment, tortious interference, and c.93A; MAPFRE seeks damages and injunctive relief. Under the JMA, AAA may place up to 50% of its business with other carriers, but those carriers may not use the AAA emblem or give AAA-member discounts [5][6].
- **Plymouth Rock** was effectively built on §193R group marketing in the 1980s–90s and remains a heavy user of group deviations and employer/association channels (7.88% share, December 2025) [10][21]. **GEICO and Safety** also maintain approved deviations [21]. The clear read: group deviations are a proven, regulator-blessed way to deliver a price advantage that the rating-factor bans otherwise make hard to engineer — which is exactly why the AAA franchise (and its scheduled expiry) is existential for MAPFRE's MA growth math.

## 5. Telematics / UBI in Massachusetts

- There is **no MA statute or regulation prohibiting telematics rating**; UBI programs operate as filed discounts within the managed-competition framework, subject to the 211 CMR 79.04 prohibited-factor list and the excessive/inadequate/unfairly-discriminatory standard [8]. We found no published DOI cap on telematics discount size (unsourced beyond carriers' own advertised maxima — flagging as an open item).
- **MAPFRE/Commerce "DriveAdvisor"** launched December 2013 — an early, device-based program aimed at drivers with **fewer than three years' experience** (plus some higher-risk experienced drivers), offering feedback and a premium discount [14][15]. Public materials do not evidence a modern, app-based, fully price-integrated successor — a gap versus competitors.
- **Plymouth Rock** markets tiered telematics in MA: **DriveSavely** (discount up to ~30%) and the **YUBI** app (advertised savings up to 40%), as of its 2026 site [16].
- **Progressive Snapshot is available in MA** (MA is not on Progressive's exclusion list of AK/HI/LA/NC/NY/OK, per Progressive program details, 2026) [23] — and Progressive is the fastest-growing carrier in the state (5.1% → 12.17% share, 2020–2025) [10].
- **State Farm's Drive Safe & Save is *not* offered in MA** (also excluded: CA, RI) — evidence that some national programs have not cleared or not attempted the MA filing environment [24].
- **Regulatory constraints on the horizon:** (i) the MA **Data Access Law** (2020 right-to-repair ballot initiative) requires telematics-equipped vehicles sold in MA to expose mechanical data via an open platform; after years of litigation, the Auto Innovators trade group and the MA AG agreed in **February 2026** to explore settlement [25]; (ii) the **Massachusetts Data Privacy Act** — House version **H.5479 passed unanimously June 4, 2026**, now being reconciled with the Senate's S.2619 — would impose consent/data-minimization duties relevant to telematics data collection [22]. Neither currently bans insurance telematics, but both raise compliance stakes and could constrain data acquisition models.

**Implication:** with credit, age, gender, occupation and education all banned, telematics is the *only* scalable, legally available behavioral segmentation tool in Massachusetts. It is also a retention and loss-control tool. MAPFRE's 2013-vintage program versus Plymouth Rock's 30–40% headline discounts and Progressive's Snapshot is a visible sophistication gap.

## 6. Pending / recent regulatory changes relevant to growth

| Item | Status (as of July 2026) | Growth relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimum limits increase to 25/50/30** (from 20/40/5) — H.5111, signed Dec 2024 | **In force July 1, 2025** for new and renewing policies [18][19] | Raised premium on minimum-limit business (~36% on average minimum-coverage premium per Bankrate estimate, from ~$524 to ~$711/yr) [19]; disproportionately affects urban/price-sensitive segments and MAIP-adjacent risks; a tailwind to written premium, a headwind to affordability and shopping behavior |
| **Auto body labor rate reform** — Advisory Board created by §292 of Ch. 238, Acts of 2024 (Mass Leads Act) | Board's final report issued **Dec 22, 2025 with no consensus recommendation**; insurer-paid rates average ~$46–49/hr, shop advocates cite benchmarks near $100/hr; live bills would mandate a floor of **at least $55/hr** [20][26][27] | Direct severity risk to physical damage loss costs for all MA writers; MAPFRE, as the largest writer, has the largest absolute exposure; watch the 2026 session |
| **MA Data Privacy Act** (H.5479 passed House 6/4/2026; Senate S.2619) | In House–Senate reconciliation [22] | Consent/data-handling requirements for telematics and digital marketing |
| **Right-to-repair / Data Access Law litigation** | AG and Auto Innovators exploring settlement (Feb 2026) [25] | Shapes access to vehicle-generated data that could feed UBI and claims automation |
| **Rating-factor regime** | No pending change found to the credit/age/gender bans or to managed competition itself (searched through July 2026) | The MA moat around incumbent, agent-based carriers persists |
| **MAIP credit rules** | DOI denied CAR's phase-out proposals in 2016 and 2019; CAR's Actuarial Committee voted in April 2025 to leave take-out credits unchanged; committees continue to monitor MAIP volume [17][13] | Credit system is stable; strategy should assume it stays |
| **Distribution / digital binding** | No MA rule found that prohibits direct online binding — GEICO, Progressive and USAA (28%+ combined share, Dec 2025 [10]) all sell direct in MA | MAPFRE's lack of a functioning direct channel is a company choice/capability gap, not a regulatory constraint |

## 7. So what for MAPFRE's growth strategy

1. **The regulatory moat is real but eroding in effect.** The credit/age/gender bans neutralize the national direct writers' biggest pricing weapons, which is a key reason Commerce still leads with 18.33% share (Dec 2025). But Commerce has shed ~4 points of share since 2020 while Progressive rose from 5.1% to 12.17% [10] — proof that expense ratio, brand, direct distribution and telematics can beat the moat. The moat buys time; it is not a strategy.

2. **Fix the MAIP asymmetry — it is a quantifiable margin leak.** Carrying 30.4% of MAIP on an 18.1% voluntary share [11] means MAPFRE absorbs a disproportionate slice of the worst risks at capped rates while competitors offset quotas with urban-territory credits. The DOI has twice refused to change the rules [17]; the actionable play is offense: build voluntary appetite and adequately priced products for the 11 credit territories (urban Boston, Springfield, Lawrence, Brockton, etc.), harvest credits and take-out credits, and treat credit generation as an explicit P&L lever. This converts a subsidy paid to competitors into growth in underpenetrated urban markets.

3. **The AAA franchise is a 2026 burning platform.** More than $200M of annual premium sits on a JMA expiring December 31, 2026, with the partner already selling against MAPFRE through MCIC [5][6]. The growth plan must model (a) litigation/renewal outcomes, (b) a retention program for AAA-sourced policyholders independent of the emblem, and (c) replacement affinity partnerships — §193R group deviations are the one regulator-sanctioned way to deliver headline price advantages in MA, and the annual DOI approval cycle (filings early each calendar year) sets the clock [21].

4. **Telematics is the only remaining sophistication frontier — and MAPFRE is behind.** With every demographic/financial variable banned, granular risk selection in MA runs through driving behavior data. DriveAdvisor (2013, device-based, new-driver-focused) [14][15] needs replacement with a modern app-based program with material, filed discounts, benchmarked against Plymouth Rock's 30–40% headline savings [16] and Snapshot [23]. Pending privacy legislation argues for building consent-first architecture now [22].

5. **Direct/digital binding is unregulated white space for MAPFRE.** Nothing in the MA regime prevents online bind; direct writers hold well over a quarter of the market [10]. Given 80% revenue concentration in MA personal auto via independent agents, even a defensive "agent-friendly digital" model (online quote-to-bind routed to agents of record) would address the channel where Progressive and GEICO are taking share.

6. **Watch loss-cost regulation, not rate suppression.** The DOI has demonstrably allowed double-digit rate increases when justified (12%+ average in 2023; GEICO ~26%) [3]. The bigger regulatory threats to margin are severity-side: the new 25/50/30 minimums (in force) [18][19] and a potential statutory auto body labor-rate floor ($55/hr bills pending; advocacy benchmarks near $100/hr vs. ~$46–49/hr paid today) [20][26][27]. MAPFRE, as the largest PPA writer, should lead industry advocacy here and pre-build the rate-filing response.

### Open items we could not resolve from public sources
- Market-wide average size of approved group deviations (only per-insurer DOI postings exist) [21].
- Any formal DOI cap or guidance specific to telematics discount magnitude.
- Current MAIP rate level vs. average voluntary rates (CAR bulletin 1175 detail not publicly summarized) [13].
- MAIP assignment tenure rules post-2014 (the consumer-facing sources no longer state a fixed assignment period) [12].
