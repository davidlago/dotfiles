# Direct and Digital Distribution: Economics and Hybrid Models
### Research brief for MAPFRE Insurance (U.S.) growth strategy — prepared July 2026

**Scope.** This brief covers (1) the economics of direct-to-consumer (DTC) personal auto, (2) what it takes to stand up bind-online capability, (3) hybrid agency+direct models and their lessons, (4) what insurtechs have proven and disproven, and (5) embedded insurance and lead aggregators as a middle path. All figures carry as-of dates and inline citations to the numbered Sources list.

---

## 1. The economics of direct-to-consumer personal auto

### 1.1 Channel-level economics: Progressive is the only clean natural experiment

Progressive is the one large carrier that publicly reports personal auto results split between an independent-agency channel and a direct channel, making it the best primary-source evidence on relative channel economics. From its September 2025 monthly earnings release (year-to-date, nine months ended 9/30/2025) [2]:

| Personal Lines – Vehicles (YTD Sept 2025) | Agency | Direct |
|---|---|---|
| Net premiums written | $22.68B (+12% YoY) | $30.05B (+20% YoY) |
| Loss/LAE ratio | 64.7 | 67.3 |
| Expense ratio | 20.3 | 22.3 |
| Combined ratio | **85.0** | **89.6** |
| Auto policies in force (9/30/25) | 10.63M (+13% YoY) | 15.62M (+17% YoY) |

Three observations:

- **Direct is not automatically cheaper.** In a heavy-growth year, Progressive's direct channel ran a *higher* expense ratio (22.3 vs. 20.3) and a higher loss ratio than agency, because advertising is expensed immediately while agent commissions are deferred over the policy term, and because direct-acquired new business skews toward higher-loss-ratio, lower-tenure customers [2][33]. S&P Global's insurtech research shows Progressive's direct auto expense ratio swung from 11.0% (July 2023, when ad spend was throttled) to 22.4% (August 2024, full growth mode) — i.e., the direct expense ratio is essentially a discretionary growth dial, not a fixed cost structure [32][33].
- **Direct wins on lifetime economics at scale, not on year-one economics.** Once a direct book seasons, renewal-year expense loads fall toward servicing cost only (no renewal commission), which is the structural source of GEICO's historically ~10-point expense-ratio advantage (see below).
- **Progressive's agency channel still grew 12-13% in 2025 even while the company poured record money into direct** — evidence the two channels can coexist inside one carrier (Section 3).

### 1.2 Advertising spend of the big direct writers

- **Progressive:** advertising expenditure hit a record of nearly **$3.5 billion in 2024** per S&P Global's analysis of filings (up 186.8% from $1.22 billion in 2023 on S&P's statutory basis; Progressive's own shareholder letter describes a ~150% increase in 2024 ad spend — the difference is methodological, and I treat the S&P figure as the better cross-carrier comparator) [1][3a]. Spend continued at ~$1.2–1.3 billion *per quarter* through 2025 (Q1 2025: $1.3B, +86% YoY; Q2: $1.2B, +35%; Q3: $1.3B, +10%) [4]. CEO Tricia Griffith: "Our operating goal is to continue to grow as fast as we can, and advertising is a great lever" [4].
- **GEICO:** cut advertising to **$838.2 million in 2023** — a 14-year low in dollars and a 26-year low as a share of direct premiums written — during its profitability turnaround [5]. It is re-ramping: projected full-year 2025 ad spend of roughly **$1.9 billion, ~35% above 2024** (implying 2024 spend of roughly $1.4 billion) [4]. Notably, S&P and Carrier Management both flag that GEICO's higher 2025 acquisition spend has *not* yet produced commensurate premium growth (Q3 2025 premium growth ~5%) [4][32] — a caution that ad dollars do not mechanically buy share in a market where Progressive is outspending everyone.
- **Scale context:** Progressive's 2024 results — net premiums written $74.4B (+21%), direct channel NPW +27%, agency +21%, companywide combined ratio 88.8, net income $8.5B [3] — show that a ~$3.5B ad budget is roughly 4-5% of premium. GEICO's 2024: premiums earned rose 7.6% (~$3.0B) to ~$42.9B, pre-tax underwriting profit $7.81B (+114.9%), **combined ratio 81.5** with a loss/LAE ratio of 71.8 — implying an expense ratio near 9.7, which Berkshire itself flagged as unusually low versus GEICO's normal level [6][7]. GEICO's Q3 2025 expense ratio was back up to 12.8 as ad spend resumed [4].

### 1.3 Acquisition cost per customer by channel

The most-cited channel CAC study (2014, so treat as directional only — no comparably granular public update exists): direct insurers (GEICO, Progressive) paid an average of **~$487 to acquire a customer**, captive-agent insurers **~$792**, and the average rose to **~$900 when independent agents were included** [9]. McKinsey's global study of direct insurance economics (July 2020) found median direct players run **sales/sales-support cost ratios >70% lower** than multichannel peers but **marketing cost ratios 5-6x higher**, are ~3x more productive in policies issued per employee, and still land at lower total cost per unit of GWP [8]. I could not source a current (2023-2026) U.S. per-policy CAC figure by channel from a primary source; the honest formulation is: **direct trades a ~12-15% recurring commission load for a large upfront media/technology load, and only wins if retention is high enough to amortize it.**

### 1.4 Channel shares

- Independent agencies placed **61.5% of total U.S. P&C premium in 2024** but only **32% of personal auto** — their lowest penetration among major lines (Big "I" 2025 Market Share Report, published July 2025) [10]. Personal-lines IA share has been *rising*: 35.7% (2020) → 39.0% (2024) [10], because national carriers pulled back and regional/IA carriers took rate more successfully in the hard market.
- Nationally (NAIC 2024 direct premiums written): State Farm 18.87% ($67.8B), Progressive 16.73% ($60.1B), Berkshire/GEICO 11.63% ($41.8B), Allstate 10.19%, USAA 6.17% [11]. The two big advertisers (Progressive + GEICO) alone hold ~28% of the national market.
- **Massachusetts:** Commerce/MAPFRE remains #1 with **18.22% of private passenger auto exposures (624,158) as of August 2025**, but **Progressive (11.92%) overtook GEICO (11.56%) in May 2025**, and the two direct writers together now hold **~23.5% of MA exposures** (total market 3.43M exposures) [12]. Since managed competition began in April 2008 (MA was the last state to deregulate rates), direct writers have compounded steadily: GEICO went from entry (2009) to 7.45% (2016) to 9.67% (2018) to ~11.6% (2025); Progressive from 3.5% (2017) to ~11.9% (2025) [12][13][14]. Agency Checklists attributes Progressive's MA gains to "policy acquisition momentum through its agency network *and* digital platforms" [12] — i.e., the hybrid attack is what is eroding Commerce's lead.

## 2. What it takes to stand up bind-online capability

**Regulatory/licensing.** Selling direct still requires licensed producers: every human who quotes/sells must hold a resident or non-resident producer license with the right line of authority in the customer's state, plus a carrier appointment [31a]. For MAPFRE this is a modest burden — an MA-centric direct operation needs MA-licensed inside sales staff (or a licensed BPO), TCPA-compliant outbound practices, and state-aware call routing if it expands to its other 18 states [31]. A purely self-service bind flow (no human) avoids per-seat licensing friction but in practice conversion collapses without licensed phone support: industry research consistently shows most shoppers research online but a large share still want a human at the point of purchase (Plymouth Rock cited 73% shop online / 60% prefer speaking to someone at purchase) [19].

**Technology.** The functional stack: rating engine exposed via API, real-time third-party data prefill (MVR, CLUE, credit where allowed — note MA bans credit and gender in auto rating, which actually *simplifies* a quote flow), online payment/e-signature, policy issuance, and a service layer (portal + app + licensed contact center). Cost benchmarks (2025-2026 vendor-benchmark and analyst estimates, directional):

| Approach | Indicative cost | Timeline |
|---|---|---|
| Full legacy replatform (Guidewire InsuranceSuite at a ~$5B-premium carrier) | $14M–$42M over 5 years incl. implementation; $3.2M–$8.4M/yr recurring post go-live [29] | 2–4 years [30] |
| Duck Creek | ~18–28% below Guidewire headline pricing, similar implementation load [29] | 1–3 years |
| Cloud-native digital front end (Socotra, BriteCore, EIS) for a DTC product, keeping the legacy PAS for the agency book | Subscription ~28–42% below Guidewire/Duck Creek at equivalent scope [29] | 3–6 months for an MVP product [30] |

The strategic implication: **MAPFRE does not need to replatform Commerce's core systems to bind online.** The proven pattern (used by Clearcover, Root, and Plymouth Rock's Prime) is a modern quote-bind-service layer wrapped around the existing rating and policy plumbing, launched on one product in one state.

**Service model.** A direct book requires 7-day licensed sales/service coverage. Outsourced U.S. licensed-agent capacity runs roughly **$48–$65 per productive hour** from insurance BPOs, which lets a carrier pilot direct without building a permanent contact center [31]. Claims, billing, and endorsements can ride on existing Commerce operations.

**Realistic all-in cost range.** No public carrier discloses a clean "cost to launch direct" number. Triangulating from the platform benchmarks above plus staffing: a single-state MVP bind-online capability is plausibly a **$5–15M build-plus-first-year proposition** (digital front end, integrations, licensed inside sales pod, compliance), *excluding* media. The media is the real cost: at Progressive-style economics, meaningful direct growth costs 4-6%+ of targeted premium in sustained advertising [1][4] — which is why Section 3's hybrid models matter more to MAPFRE than a GEICO-style frontal assault.

## 3. Hybrid agency+direct models: what worked, what blew up

### 3.1 Progressive: the canonical case

Progressive built its IA business first, launched direct (1-800-AUTO-PRO, then online) in the 1990s, and in **September 2004 split its brands** — "Drive Insurance from Progressive" for its ~30,000 independent agents, "Progressive Direct" for phone/web — with each channel making independent pricing and product decisions [15][16]. By **February 2007 it killed the Drive brand** after research showed "the power of the Progressive name" was the agents' biggest asset; ~15,000 Drive signs were replaced with Progressive-branded signage, and agents publicly welcomed the move [16]. Durable design choices that survived: separate rate filings/pricing by channel (each channel carries its own acquisition cost), agents keep ownership of their book, and one consumer brand. Result today: both channels growing double digits simultaneously (Section 1.1) [2]. **Lesson: the brand can be shared; the economics must be channel-separated; agents tolerate a direct channel when the carrier's brand pulls business into their offices too.**

### 3.2 Safeco / Liberty Mutual: dual brands as channel insulation — now ended

Liberty Mutual ran a two-brand system for 18 years: Safeco (acquired 2008) exclusively for ~22,000 independent agencies (~$14B annual premium), Liberty Mutual for direct/captive [17]. In March 2025 it announced, and by **April 25, 2026 completed, retirement of the Safeco brand**, promising agents that IA-channel and direct products "will remain differentiated," with agents continuing to own the customer relationship [17][17a]. Trade coverage notes the obvious tension: agents now sell the same brand the carrier advertises direct [17b]. **Lesson: a separate agent-facing brand can buy peace for years, but it is expensive to maintain and tends to be consolidated once the direct brand dominates awareness — at which point the carrier must offer structural (product/ownership) rather than cosmetic (brand) protections to agents.**

### 3.3 Plymouth Rock "Prime": the most MAPFRE-relevant template

In **August 2015**, Plymouth Rock — a direct MA/CT/NH competitor of Commerce — launched **Prime**: consumers quote, buy, and manage the policy fully online at plymouthrock.com, and **every policy sold online is then transferred to a local independent agent**, who pays a one-time marketing fee, thereafter **owns the business for life and receives full commission**; abandoned online quotes are handed to agents as leads [18][19]. Plymouth Rock positioned itself as "the only auto insurance company in Massachusetts, Connecticut and New Hampshire to give consumers the ability to buy and manage a policy online *and* the benefit of in-person counsel through an independent agent" [18]. A decade later Plymouth Rock still markets online, phone, and agent channels side by side and holds ~8.0% of MA auto exposures (Aug 2025) [12][20a]. **Lesson: in an agent-dense state like Massachusetts, "direct-to-agent" — carrier-funded digital acquisition with post-sale assignment of the policy to an agent at full or near-full commission — captures the digital shopper without declaring war on the channel. This is the closest existing blueprint for MAPFRE.**

### 3.4 Hippo's builders channel: distribution partnerships as the growth engine

Hippo, a DTC-born home insurtech, found its fastest growth not online but in the **New Homes Program** — embedded insurance at the point of home purchase through homebuilders — which became "a substantial majority" of its new business in key states [35]. In June 2025 it **sold the homebuilder distribution network (~$29.2M TTM revenue) to Baldwin Group's Westwood**, which post-close serves 20 of the top 25 U.S. homebuilders (>35% of new single-family homes), while Hippo retained the risk/underwriting role via long-term commercial agreements [20]. **Lesson: even a celebrated DTC insurtech concluded that (a) point-of-need partner distribution beats paid digital acquisition, and (b) owning distribution and owning underwriting are separable businesses.**

## 4. What insurtechs proved and disproved about digital auto

**Root (telematics-first DTC auto).** After burning enormous capital on paid acquisition post-IPO (2020), Root restructured and reached **net income of $30.9M in 2024 (combined ratio 96.4)** and **record net income of $40.3M in 2025 (combined ratio 98.2)**, with policies in force growing from 414,862 to **481,869** at year-end 2025 [21]. Two strategically telling facts: Root's growth now leans on **partnerships (Carvana embedded at car purchase; a Toyota/Lexus connected-vehicle telematics integration launched Q4 2025)**, and its CEO called **independent agents Root's "fastest growing segment"**: "We have not seen demand in this channel slow, as we continue to appoint more of the market" [21]. A digital-native carrier is *adding* the agency channel — the mirror image of MAPFRE's problem.

**Lemonade (brand-led digital, auto as expansion line).** Lemonade Car reached only **~$187M in-force premium at year-end 2025** — about 15% of Lemonade's $1.24B total IFP — despite availability in states representing ~42% of the U.S. auto market and a claimed 700k-customer cross-sell waitlist [22][23][24]. The company remains loss-making (Q4 2025 net loss $21.7M) even as its gross loss ratio improved to 52% in Q4 2025 [22][22a]. **Auto has proven much harder for Lemonade than renters/pet: brand-led digital demand does not transfer cheaply into a high-premium, telematics-priced, regulated line.**

**Clearcover (API-first, distribution-agnostic).** Clearcover pivoted from pure DTC to **embedded/API distribution**: bindable final quotes inside **Experian's** comparison service (April 2023) and a **quote-and-bind API inside Goosehead Insurance's agent quoting platform** (October 2023) [25][26]. **Lesson: the durable insurtech asset turned out to be the quote-bind API, deployable in someone else's channel — including agents' desktops — not the consumer brand.**

**Net verdict — proven:** digital quote-bind UX materially lowers unit servicing cost and is now table-stakes for shoppers; telematics-at-quote can price risk (Root's sub-100 combined ratios); embedded/point-of-sale placement converts far better than paid search. **Disproven:** that a better app plus performance marketing profitably out-acquires incumbents at scale (Root's 2020-2022 losses, Lemonade Car's slow build); that "digital" and "agent" are opposing strategies (Root and Clearcover both now court agents); that brand affection substitutes for price competitiveness in auto.

## 5. The middle path: lead aggregators and embedded insurance

**Online aggregators (EverQuote, Zebra, Insurify, MediaAlpha, QuoteWizard).** These are marketplaces where carriers and agents buy consumer intent — clicks, leads, and calls, cleared mostly at fixed cost-per-lead in real-time ping-post auctions [28a]. The scale is large: **EverQuote's FY2024 revenue was $500.2M (+74% YoY), of which the auto vertical was $446.1M (+96%)** [27]; one 2024 industry analysis pegs total U.S. carrier/agent spend on third-party auto insurance leads, calls, and clicks at roughly **$3.5B** (broader definitions $5.2–6.8B — single-source estimate, moderate confidence) [28]. Aggregator volumes boom exactly when carriers reopen growth appetite (EverQuote's Q3 2024 revenue +163% YoY as loss ratios normalized) [27a]. For a carrier without a bind-online flow, aggregator traffic can be routed to licensed inside sales or to appointed agents — a way to buy digital shoppers *without* building GEICO-scale brand advertising. The risk: aggregator CPLs are auction-priced, so a sub-scale buyer with weak conversion funnels overpays; Progressive's own direct machine is increasingly expected to squeeze aggregator economics [28b].

**Embedded insurance.** Market-size forecasts diverge wildly ($14.9B in 2025 [34a] vs. $143.9B in 2025 [34b] globally, definitions differ) and should not be load-bearing; the observable facts are the deals: Tesla and Toyota embed insurance in the purchase flow via Sure's infrastructure; Toyota's telematics data feeds a Nationwide-backed UBI offer; Root embeds in Carvana checkout and Toyota/Lexus vehicles; Hippo/Westwood embeds at 20 of the top 25 homebuilders [20][21][34c]. Embedded auto in the U.S. remains early and OEM-gated — relevant to MAPFRE mainly through affinity/bank/dealer partnerships in its footprint rather than OEM deals it cannot win at its scale.

## 6. So what for MAPFRE's growth strategy

1. **Do not attempt a GEICO/Progressive-style DTC frontal assault.** The entry ticket is national-scale advertising ($1.9–3.5B/yr for the leaders in 2024-2025 [1][4]) and multi-year tolerance of a 20%+ expense-ratio channel [2]. At Commerce's ~$2B scale, an economically meaningful media budget (4-6% of premium) would buy a rounding error of share in an ad market Progressive is deliberately flooding.

2. **The status quo is the riskier option.** Direct writers already hold ~23.5% of MA auto exposures and Progressive — running the hybrid playbook — passed GEICO in May 2025 and is compounding while Commerce's share drifts (19% in Oct 2024 → 18.2% in Aug 2025) [12]. Every year without bind-online cedes the fastest-growing shopper segment.

3. **Build "direct-to-agent," not "direct-vs-agent" — the Plymouth Rock Prime model is the proof it works in Massachusetts specifically.** Let customers quote and bind on mapfreinsurance.com, then assign the policy to a local partner agent at full or lightly-reduced commission, with the agent owning the renewal [18][19]. This converts the ~4,200-agent network from an obstacle into the reason the strategy is safe: agents gain digitally-originated customers; MAPFRE gains the 30-40% of shoppers who will not walk into an agency. Progressive's Drive episode adds the branding lesson: keep one brand (MAPFRE/Commerce equity), separate the channel economics in filings [16][2].

4. **The build is affordable because the scope is narrow.** One state, one product, no credit-based rating variables to integrate, existing claims/service operations: a cloud front-end wrapped around Commerce's rating engine is a months-not-years, single-digit-millions MVP [29][30][31], with licensed BPO capacity ($48-65/hr) as the variable-cost sales pod [31].

5. **Buy digital demand before building digital brand.** Participate in EverQuote/MediaAlpha/Insurify auctions and Experian-style embedded placements with bindable quotes routed to the new flow or to agents — the Clearcover/Root pattern [25][26][21]. This yields immediate volume, funnel data, and CAC benchmarks while the bind-online capability matures, at a fraction of brand-advertising cost.

6. **Measure the channel on lifetime economics with channel-separated pricing.** Progressive's data warns that year one of a direct book looks ugly (higher expense and loss ratios) [2][32][33]; the business case must be underwritten on retention-adjusted lifetime CAC vs. the ~12-15% perpetual commission alternative, not on year-one combined ratio.

**Open items we could not resolve from public sources:** a current (2023-2026) per-policy CAC by channel from a primary source; Plymouth Rock's Prime volume, marketing-fee level, and channel P&L; the actual dollar cost any carrier incurred to launch bind-online; Commerce/MAPFRE's own channel economics (internal data required); and Massachusetts-specific direct-vs-agency loss-ratio experience (CAR data does not split by sales channel publicly).
