# Massachusetts Customers: Demographics, Segments, and Trends
### Research brief for MAPFRE Insurance U.S. growth strategy — customer fact base
*Prepared July 2026. All figures carry as-of dates; sources are numbered and listed at the end.*

---

## 1. Executive summary

Massachusetts is a slow-growing, aging, increasingly diverse state whose entire net population growth now depends on international immigration — above all Latin American and Brazilian immigration. The state added only ~15,500 residents in the year to June 30, 2025 (+0.2%), as net international migration halved from 78,000 to 40,000 and 33,000 residents left for other states [2]. Latinos accounted for roughly 8 of every 10 net new Massachusetts residents from 2014-2023 [6][7], and Brazilians are the state's largest immigrant group [10]. At the same time, the customer base is aging fast (every MA county hit its all-time-high 65+ share in 2024 [1]), vehicles are getting more expensive to repair as ADAS spreads (calibrations on 23% of repairable appraisals in 2025 vs. 0.9% in 2017 [22]), and consumers nationally are shopping and switching auto insurance at record rates — with growth flowing to the direct channel (+12.6% new business in Q4 2025) while the independent-agent channel, MAPFRE's near-exclusive channel, was flat to negative (-0.1%) [25]. The strategic tension for MAPFRE is stark: its future MA customer is younger, browner, more Portuguese/Spanish-speaking, and more digital than its current book — and its Spanish-brand heritage is a genuine but so-far-unmonetized asset in reaching that customer.

## 2. Massachusetts demographic trends

### 2.1 Population growth: slow, and now almost entirely immigration-dependent

- Massachusetts reached approximately **7.15 million residents as of June 30, 2025**, up only ~15,500 (+0.2%) year over year — less than a quarter of the prior year's pace [2].
- The 2025 growth math (per Census Bureau estimates released January 2026): **net international migration +40,000** (down sharply from +78,000 in 2024), **net domestic out-migration -33,000**, **natural increase +8,400** [2]. Without immigrants, Massachusetts shrinks.
- Massachusetts had the **4th-highest rate of net international immigration per 1,000 residents** among states in 2024 estimates [5], and the foreign-born share reached **18.8% in 2024** (~1.3 million people), vs. 14.8% nationally and up from 15.7% in 2014 [5].
- Longer-run official projections (UMass Donahue Institute, produced for the Secretary of the Commonwealth; latest "Vintage 2024" series runs to 2050) show slow structural growth; the widely cited earlier UMDI series projected just **4.4% growth over 2010-2030** (6.5M to 6.8M) — actual growth has outrun that thanks to immigration, but the direction (slow growth, fast aging) is unchanged [3][4]. The national immigration slowdown that began in 2025 under new federal enforcement policy (national net international migration fell from 2.7M to 1.3M in one year) is a direct downside risk to MA's insurable-population growth [2].

**Implication:** organic policy-count growth in MA personal auto is close to zero unless a carrier wins share — and the only organically growing customer pools are immigrant and Hispanic/Brazilian communities and seniors.

### 2.2 Aging: an old state getting older

Per Census Bureau Vintage 2024 estimates (as of July 1, 2024; released June 26, 2025) [1]:

- Median age: **40.1 years** (U.S.: 39.1); 10 of 14 MA counties are at 40+.
- **18.7% of MA residents are 65+** (U.S.: 18.0%); only **19.0% are under 18** (U.S.: 21.5%).
- In 2024, **every Massachusetts county recorded its highest-ever share of population 65+** in the 2000-2024 series, and many recorded their lowest-ever share of children under 5 [1].
- Oldest counties: Barnstable (median 56.3, 34.8% aged 65+), Dukes (50.8), Berkshire (47.5). Youngest: Suffolk (34.2), Hampshire (37.0), Middlesex (39.0) [1].
- The older UMDI projection series expected the 65+ share to reach ~21% by 2030 (from 14% in 2010) [4].

### 2.3 Diversity, Hispanic/Latino growth, and geography

- MA's combined racial/ethnic minority population reached **32.4% in 2024**, up **9.3 percentage points since 2010** — faster diversification than the U.S. overall (+6.4 pts) [1].
- The **Hispanic/Latino share of MA population was 14.0% as of July 1, 2024** (Census V2024) [1] — roughly 1.0 million people. (Note: some secondary sources cite higher figures up to 18.6%; we trust the Census Bureau/UMDI 14.0% figure. The 12% figure in older Boston Indicators material is 2017-vintage [8].)
- **Latinos accounted for roughly 8 in 10 net new Massachusetts residents from 2014-2023**, growing the state's Latino population ~25% over that period, per the 2025 Massachusetts Latino Economic Report (Boston Indicators / Mass. Taxpayers Foundation / UMass Gastón Institute, published April-May 2025) [6][7]. The Latino workforce grew **49% (+148,000 workers)** over the decade — seven times the state's overall job-growth rate — and Latinos contributed **~$30 billion (~25%) of the state's economic growth** 2014-2023 [6].
- Hispanic residents are much younger than average: U.S. Hispanic median age is **31.2 vs. 41.4 for non-Hispanics** (Census V2024) [1] — i.e., this is where new drivers come from.

**County concentration of Hispanic population (July 1, 2024)** [1]:

| County | Hispanic share | Key cities | Median age |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hampden | 28.0% | Springfield (42% Latino), Holyoke (50%) [8] | 39.9 |
| Essex | 25.7% | Lawrence (77% Latino), Lynn (36%) [8] | 41.1 |
| Suffolk | 24.1% | Boston (20%), Chelsea (66%) [8] | 34.2 |

| U.S. | 20.0% | — | 39.1 |

- Origin mix (Boston, per Boston Indicators): **Puerto Rican 28%, Dominican 24%, Salvadoran 11%, Colombian 6%, Mexican 6%**; 43% of Boston Latinos are foreign-born (Puerto Ricans are citizens by birth) [8]. Western MA (Springfield/Holyoke) is heavily Puerto Rican.
- Latino growth is spreading beyond the traditional gateway cities into smaller towns: from 2018-2023 the Latino population share rose more than 5 points in nine towns of 10,000+, led by Nantucket (+11.9 pts), Winthrop (+10.9), Southbridge (+8.1) (Boston Globe analysis, Dec 2024) [9].

### 2.4 Brazilians and Portuguese speakers: the largest immigrant group, statistically invisible

- **Brazilians are Massachusetts' largest immigrant group and have been since 2010** [10]. The official count is ~**140,000 Brazilian residents**, but the October 2024 Boston Foundation / Instituto Diaspora Brasil report argues the true figure **could be roughly double** once undocumented residents and census misclassification are counted [10][11].
- MA holds **17% of all Brazilian immigrants in the U.S. (2020-24 ACS)**, second only to Florida (23%); Greater Boston is one of the top three Brazilian metro destinations nationally [12].
- Concentration: **Framingham, Everett** and surrounding Greater Boston communities (also Malden, Marlborough, Worcester per the underlying report) [10][11].
- The community skews working-age (**84% aged 18-64 in 2022**) and entrepreneurial (~24,500 Brazilian-owned businesses in construction, real estate, restaurants, cleaning/domestic services), contributing ~$8B to gross state product in 2022 [10].
- Critically for insurers: Brazilians are **Portuguese-speaking, not Spanish-speaking**, and are frequently miscounted because "Hispanic/Latino" census categories don't capture them [10]. A "Hispanic strategy" that is Spanish-only misses MA's single largest immigrant segment. Massachusetts also retains large legacy Portuguese and Cape Verdean Portuguese-speaking communities (New Bedford/Fall River corridor) [12 context; MAPS advocacy data].

## 3. Does MAPFRE's Spanish/Latin brand translate into U.S. Hispanic advantage — and what wins Hispanic customers?

### 3.1 MAPFRE's own track record

- MAPFRE S.A. is **the largest multinational insurer in Latin America** and the largest Spanish-owned insurer globally, operating in 30+ countries with ~31 million customers [16]. Brand familiarity among first-generation Latin American immigrants is plausible and was the explicit thesis of MAPFRE's 2014 California push: then-CEO Antonio Huertas said, *"People are waiting for a Hispanic insurance company. If they like to eat Latino food, they'd like to have Latino insurance products."* MAPFRE placed insurance kiosks in Latino grocery chains (Northgate González, Vallarta, El Super) targeting ~1.5M newly license-eligible undocumented drivers (LA Business Journal, April 20, 2014) [13].
- That thesis was never proven at scale: in December 2017 MAPFRE USA reorganized around profitability, exited five states, and concentrated on the Northeast, which held 67% of U.S. business; the Western region (California et al.) was just 9% and was subsequently wound down — MAPFRE today serves ~11 states with its center of gravity in Massachusetts, where it insures more homes and autos than any other carrier [14][15][16].
- **We found no public evidence that MAPFRE has ever systematically activated its Latin brand in Massachusetts** — no Spanish/Portuguese-first product, agency program, or marketing campaign specific to MA's Latino or Brazilian communities surfaced in our searches. This is a gap, not a disproof.
- One under-exploited connection: MAPFRE has long operated a major business in **Puerto Rico** (one of its four U.S. regions in the 2017 reorg) [14], and **Puerto Ricans are the largest Latino origin group in Boston (28%) and dominant in Springfield/Holyoke** [8]. Brand familiarity from the island plus MA's Puerto Rican concentration is the most direct, evidence-based bridge between MAPFRE's Latin brand and an actual MA customer segment.

### 3.2 What carriers actually do to win Hispanic insurance customers

The playbook, per industry research and practitioner sources [13][17][18][19][32]:

1. **In-language, in-culture — beyond translation.** More than half of Spanish-dominant U.S. adults prefer to make purchasing decisions in Spanish, and **~40% say they will only make high-value purchases if information and support exist in their native language** [18]. Winners run fully integrated Spanish (and for MA, Portuguese) capability across quoting, service, and claims — not just a translated landing page.
2. **Trusted-intermediary distribution.** Hispanic consumers lean on family, community, and trusted advisors; bilingual local agents and community brokers convert far better than anonymous digital funnels [17][19]. Specialist distributors (e.g., Infinity/Kemper's Hispanic-focused agency model in other states, Freeway Insurance) built entire franchises on bilingual storefronts.
3. **Community presence and events** — sponsorship of cultural events (Día de las Madres, Hispanic Heritage Month), grocery-anchored retail kiosks, church and hometown-association partnerships [13][19].
4. **Product fit** — flexible payment plans, products recognizable from home markets, and first-time-buyer education (many arrivals come from low-insurance-penetration markets; only 43% of Hispanic Americans own life insurance, the lowest of any group — a signal of general under-insurance) [17].
5. **Mobile-first digital in Spanish.** ~87% of Hispanic consumers get news digitally; cultural marketing now runs through bilingual mobile experiences and influencers, not just Spanish TV [17].

**Assessment:** MAPFRE's brand is a real but perishable asset with first-generation immigrants (and strongest with Puerto Ricans and South Americans who know the brand from home). It confers little advantage with U.S.-born second-generation Latinos, and none with Brazilians unless MAPFRE activates its Brazil business heritage (MAPFRE also operates in Brazil at scale). The advantage only materializes with deliberate investment in bilingual agents, service, and community distribution in Lawrence, Springfield, Holyoke, Chelsea, Lynn, Framingham, and Everett.

## 4. Vehicle trends in Massachusetts

### 4.1 EV adoption: growing but behind target, and stalling

- Massachusetts ended 2025 with **166,296 light-duty EVs on the road vs. a state target of 200,000** — a ~17% shortfall (Mass.gov 2025 Climate Report Card) [20]. That is still a low-single-digit share of the state's light-duty fleet (fleet denominator not published in the report card; our characterization, not a sourced ratio).
- EVs were **12.6% of new MA car registrations in Q4 2023** [21]; adoption plateaued in 2025 after the federal $7,500 tax credit was rescinded and EV sales slowed at year-end [20].
- Charging infrastructure grew fast off a small base: **10,387 public ports at end-2025 (+18%), DC fast ports +58%**, vs. an estimated need of 12,000 by end-2025 and 45,500 by 2030 [20].
- Underwriting relevance: LexisNexis finds drivers switching from gas cars to EVs show **~14% higher claim frequency** in their first EV year [26], and EV repairs skew to higher severity.

### 4.2 ADAS and repair-cost severity

Per CCC Intelligent Solutions Crash Course (Q4 2025) [22]:

- **ADAS calibrations appeared on 23% of repairable appraisals in 2025, up from 0.9% in 2017**; diagnostic scans appear on ~88% of DRP estimates.
- **Average total cost of repair rose to $4,768 through Q3 2025** (from $4,700), driven by parts prices and vehicle-technology complexity rather than macro inflation; CCC's explicit conclusion is that rising vehicle sophistication is now a primary severity driver.
- Frequency is falling while severity rises — the classic ADAS trade — muting the loss-cost benefit of safer cars. LexisNexis corroborates on the injury side: **bodily injury severity +9.2%, property damage +2.5% (2024)** [26].
- Behavioral risk is worsening: all U.S. driving violations **+17% YoY in 2024**, distracted-driving violations **+50%**, with the largest DUI increase among drivers 66-90 [26] — relevant to an aging MA book.

### 4.3 Miles driven

- MA total VMT was **~57.5 billion miles in 2024, up ~7% from 2022** and back above pre-pandemic levels [23]; state climate-plan modeling projects light-duty VMT of ~57.9B in 2025 rising to **~59.1B by 2030** even as per-household VMT declines [24]. Exposure (miles) is growing slowly; risk per mile (severity, distraction) is growing faster.

### 4.4 Massachusetts rate environment (context for shopping behavior)

- MA auto rates rose sharply in 2023-2024 (individual renewal increases reportedly as high as 60% in extreme cases; no official average published) [30 context].
- Effective **July 1, 2025, Massachusetts raised mandatory minimum liability limits** (first increase in decades); Bankrate estimated average minimum-coverage premiums would jump ~36%, from ~$524 to ~$711/year [30][31]. Rate shock is a proven shopping trigger — this event alone put a wave of MA minimum-limit customers in motion in H2 2025.

## 5. Consumer shopping behavior, price sensitivity, and digital expectations

### 5.1 Shopping and switching are at all-time highs (U.S.; MA-specific data not public)

| Metric | Value | As of | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-force policies shopped in past 12 months | **47.1%** (record) | Q4 2025 | LexisNexis [25] |
| Customers who shopped in past year (survey) | **57%** (record) | Jan-Mar 2025 | J.D. Power [28][29] |
| Quarterly switch rate | **4.5%** (all-time high) | Q3 2025 | J.D. Power [via 27 context] |
| U.S. carrier retention | **78%**, down 5 pts since 2021 | YE 2024 | LexisNexis [26] |
| New-business growth, direct channel | **+12.6% YoY** | Q4 2025 | LexisNexis [25] |
| New-business growth, exclusive agents | +5.3% YoY | Q4 2025 | LexisNexis [25] |
| New-business growth, **independent agents** | **-0.1% YoY** | Q4 2025 | LexisNexis [25] |

- Even historically loyal segments are in motion: **consumers 66+ grew shopping 11% YoY (fastest of any cohort)**, and shopping by 10+ year tenured customers rose 35% YoY [25][26]. Rate-driven price sensitivity has spread from young/high-risk segments to seniors and bundlers [27].
- **No Massachusetts-specific shop/switch rate is published** in public LexisNexis/J.D. Power material. MA has historically been considered a lower-shopping, high-loyalty state (group/affinity marketing, agent relationships), but we could not source a current MA figure — flagged as an open question. The July 2025 minimum-limits repricing [30][31] almost certainly pushed MA shopping up in 2025-26.

### 5.2 Digital expectations by cohort

- **47% of auto insurance shoppers now buy through digital channels vs. 35% through agents and 17% through call centers** (J.D. Power 2025 U.S. Insurance Digital Experience Study, fielded Jan-Mar 2025) [28][29].
- Digital experience quality is decisive: where digital-shopping satisfaction exceeded 801/1,000, 92% of customers said they'd continue using digital channels; below 500, only 40% [28].
- Younger cohorts are the least loyal and most price-stressed: retention declines were **largest among Millennials (-12 points)** [29 context via study coverage], and **43% of Gen Z customers hit with price increases moved to deductibles of $1,000+** [29]. Interest in embedded insurance (dealer/OEM-provided) is highest among Gen Y/Z at 47% — a structural threat to both agents and traditional carriers [29].
- Older shoppers still favor agents and phone but are now shopping actively [25]; the agent channel remains essential for complex needs and for immigrant/LEP customers who need in-language help [17][18].

## 6. So what for MAPFRE's growth strategy

1. **The MA market grows only where MAPFRE isn't looking.** Net customer formation in Massachusetts is concentrated in Latino and Brazilian immigrant communities (8 in 10 net new residents [6]) and in the 65+ cohort. MAPFRE's 80%-MA, independent-agent book skews to the aging, slow-shrinking legacy population. Holding share of a static pool is a melting-ice-cube strategy; share of *new-resident* household formation is the growth pool.
2. **The Latin brand is an option MAPFRE has never exercised in its own backyard.** The 2014 California experiment showed intent but was abandoned for profitability reasons [13][14]. In MA, the highest-probability version is narrower and cheaper: (a) a Puerto Rico-brand bridge into Springfield/Holyoke/Boston's Puerto Rican and Dominican communities; (b) a Portuguese-language offering for the Brazilian corridor (Framingham-Everett-Malden-Worcester), where no carrier owns the segment and MAPFRE operates at scale in Brazil itself. Requirements: bilingual/trilingual service and claims end-to-end (40% of Spanish-dominant consumers won't buy high-value products without native-language support [18]), recruitment of Latino- and Brazilian-owned agencies in gateway cities, and community-anchored marketing — not translation alone [17][19].
3. **The channel math is a strategic emergency.** Nationally, direct-channel new business grew 12.6% in Q4 2025 while independent agents shrank 0.1% [25], and 47% of buyers now close digitally [29]. With no bindable direct/digital path, MAPFRE is structurally absent from the channel where nearly half of record-high shopping volume converts — including the digital-first second-generation Latino and Gen Z/Millennial customers it needs. Minimum viable move: digital bind (even agent-assigned post-sale to preserve the agent franchise), in English, Spanish, and Portuguese.
4. **Record shopping cuts both ways.** 47-57% of customers shopping [25][29] plus the July 2025 MA minimum-limits repricing [30][31] means MAPFRE's large in-force book is maximally exposed to defection right now — retention analytics, renewal-shock management, and senior-segment care (66+ is the fastest-growing shopper cohort [25]) are as valuable as acquisition.
5. **Severity, not frequency, is the loss-cost story.** ADAS calibrations (23% of appraisals [22]), $4,768 average repair cost [22], +9.2% BI severity [26], distracted driving +50% [26], and early-tenure EV frequency +14% [26] argue for repair-network depth, calibration capability, and EV-specific pricing as quiet competitive weapons in a state pushing (however haltingly) toward 200k+ EVs [20].
6. **Watch the immigration-policy tail risk.** MA population growth is now a single-factor bet on international migration, which halved in 2025 [2]. A multi-year federal clampdown shrinks MAPFRE's only growing segment — one more reason the realistic growth agenda must combine new-segment acquisition with share capture from competitors via digital capability and retention.
